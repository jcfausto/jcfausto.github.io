Hiring great engineers is less about matching a résumé to a technology stack and more about understanding how a person will learn, adapt, collaborate, and contribute.

I found an old note from March 2022 about “How to hire great software engineers,” reread it four years later, and was surprised by how much—and how little—my thinking had changed.

**The topics are:**

1. Culture fit
2. Experience fit
3. Technical skills fit
4. Diversity

# Culture Fit

Originally, I wrote:

"Culture fit (I love this goal of not hiring "assholes"), I've worked with some in my career and I know that despite the fact that some are really good at what they do technically, they produce more harm than good to the team and the organization (I think it's the idea of optimizing locally (having an IC that's great technically, but missing the global perspective of the team and organization morale and culture)."

I don't think much differently today. By cultural fit, I mean alignment in behaviors and values rather than similarity in personality. I still believe that hiring for cultural fit should be a priority for all companies. No matter how  things have changed with the AI era in which we are in, cultural fit is still one of the most important predictors of success for an employee.

It is no coincidence that I've been working for almost four years on this problem as part of my current job. How to increase the chances of placing the right people in the right roles with a strong focus on cultural fit has been a constant challenge for me, reflected in solutions created in our product to try to solve or at least mitigate this problem.

A person, no matter their position in the company, that does not fit the organization's social fabric, may struggle to thrive and is more likely to leave. But before that, a long period of suffering and wasted time may follow. Since this is usually not quantified, companies don't pay too much attention to it, but the indirect costs associated with that are high.

Team morale is the first hidden cost. Working for a long period of time with a person who does not fit your working environment and agreed social contracts can be stressful and exhausting. Another hidden cost falls on that person's line manager, who will have to deal with constant conflict resolution. This can be demanding and take focus away from higher-value work.

Don't get me wrong. I'm not advocating against diversity. That's something I truly value, as you'll see below. What I'm referring to is about people that disrupt the team and turn it into a dysfunctional unit. These people should be let go as soon as possible as staying longer won't be good either for the team - which will not perform at its best - or the individual, which will waste time in their careers working on a place they don't like.

Cultural fit must not be a euphemism for homogeneity, and it shouldn't mean hiring people who think like you. Instead, it should mean hiring people who can work constructively within the team's values while bringing different experiences, perspectives, and ideas.

# Experience Fit

Originally, I wrote:

"Experience fit: Has this candidate gone through similar experiences before? What did they learn?"

Understanding the kinds of experiences a person has had in previous jobs can help you understand them much better. Through their experiences you'll get a clearer picture of their exposure to different kinds of problems and levels of complexity. Most importantly, you'll be able to understand what they learned from these experiences, because that's the baggage they are bringing to the team. This is what they'll use as a starting point when they join the company. It is their personal body of knowledge that will mix with the team's over time.

If a big gap exists between their past experiences and the company's day-to-day reality, there will be a clash and tension that sometimes can be resolved and sometimes they cannot. It will always depend on how that person collaborates, how open they are to change, and how open you and your team are to change as well. Change is good when it gets you closer to where you want to be. It isn't good when it moves you backwards or takes you further from your goal.

# Technical Skills Fit

Originally, I wrote:

"Technical skills fit? Does this candidate have the basics in place? The rest the candidate can learn either from us or from training we will support/offer to the candidate."

At the time, I already believed that matching a candidate to a specific technology stack was one of the less critical parts of hiring. Strong engineers can learn quickly when they have solid foundations and the right support. Today, I believe this even more strongly.

Of course you'll have to understand your needs and if you can afford the time that the person would take to learn something new, but in general, technical skills are not that critical anymore. An engineer with relevant experience in similar technologies can pick up a new tech stack very quickly.

More important than matching specific technologies is understanding how a person solves problems and how they approach them. The lack of knowledge on a certain technology is a problem, and getting to know how that person could address that problem is very relevant because you may have a wide spectrum of behaviours here. You might have someone that will delegate that responsibility to you and the organization and will wait for you to teach them, or you might have a person who will say "I've got it" and will learn what needs to be learned by themselves with little supervision (I personally prefer the latter, people who take ownership of closing their own knowledge gaps).

# Diversity

Originally, I wrote:

"Diversity within the different roles and seniority levels as well."

Here I clearly scoped what I meant by diversity. It is about diversity of thought, experience, and life stories. This mix is essential in any team, and as a hiring manager or someone in charge of hiring someone else, you should be aware of what that person will add to the mix.

The worst thing that can happen is for you to have or be on a team where everyone conforms and thinks the same way. Respectful disagreement and discussion of ideas are things I genuinely appreciate because they are one of the ways we learn from each other as professionals. I guess this relates to the idea of "learning to unlearn". Over the course of one's career, it is fundamental to acquire the skill of learning new things and letting old knowledge or beliefs go. In today's fast-paced technology environment, I believe this skill is more valuable than ever.

# Conclusion

Recruiting great software engineers isn't just about asking, “Does this person have the technical skills I need?” It's about finding people who can learn, adapt, collaborate, challenge ideas constructively, and add something new to the team.

Four years later, that's the part of my old list I believe even more strongly.
